# Evolve to a Viable Business

**Objective**: turn accumulated learning into a sustainable business. Once the problem,
the solution and the delivery capability are confirmed, the focus becomes **business
risk**.

The search is not for a final state called "success" but for a **dynamic equilibrium**
between four fits:

*(Framing from the article "The Intersection of Fit: Market ∩ Product ∩ Channel ∩ Model".)*

1. **Market-product fit** — the product solves a real problem and there is demand.
2. **Model-market fit** — the business model is viable and sustainable in that market.
3. **Channel-model fit** — sales, marketing and delivery channels suit the model.
4. **Product-channel fit** — channels are suitable for selling and delivering the
   product efficiently.

## Synthesize the learning with an evolutionary lens

Turning the valuable manual process into a first version is rarely a straight line. It
looks more like **crossing a river on stepping stones** — each step reveals the best
position for the next.

Rather than fixing on a destination, borrow from evolution: it does not follow a
predefined objective, it explores what is interesting from the current point. The aim is
the most promising **stepping stone** — the one that opens the largest number of new
paths.

Reflection questions for the first version:

- **Flexibility and exaptation**: does this version allow uses we did not predict? Can it
  be exapted to solve other problems later?
- **Connection potential (solution space)**: does it serve as a foundation for other
  features?
- **Ecosystem (gene flow)**: how easily does it connect to the other tools the audience
  already uses? Will it live well in a larger ecosystem?
- **Competitive advantage (local competition)**: does it position us in a specific niche
  that is hard to imitate, instead of broad direct competition?

## Translate the process into the first stepping stone

With value and commitment risks already mitigated, the decision is **what to systematize
or automate first**. Each choice optimizes a different aspect of the future business.

**Systematize the core value (the "minimum version")** — automate only the most critical
and unique part of the process, the one generating the greatest perceived value. Release
a system with a single essential feature to a small group. Tests execution risk at scale
with minimum engineering effort.

**Build the acquisition engine** — instead of the product itself, build a feature or tool
whose main purpose is to attract customers: a referral system, shareable content
generated by the product, or a free tool as a lure. Tests business-model and channel risk.

**Create an internal support tool** — build a small product that automates a painful part
of your own manual process. Raises efficiency and margin, and can eventually become a
customer-facing product.

**Develop in partnership (the "pilot solution")** — find a strategic client or partner and
co-develop the first version around their needs. Serves as an in-depth test case,
validates viability in a real scenario, can guarantee initial revenue, and mitigates the
risk of building something that does not fit a real workflow.

## While building the version

Objective: **decrease your own effort to deliver value**.

- **Systematization and automation** — evaluate AI assistance, code or low-code to
  automate the most critical parts of the process discovered in the previous stage.
- **Simplification and removal** — drop from the process (service or product) everything
  that showed no clear value for the first users.
- **Strategic partnerships** — consider partnerships to optimize or outsource parts of the
  process that are not your focus.

Revisit the nine components for a desirable solution (see `stelow-product-solutions`) to
keep this first version valuable and easy to use.

**Write one or more case studies** with your first clients — they work as social proof
and refine your communication.

## Optimize the solution experience

Using what stages 4 and 5 taught you, adjust: business model and pricing (see
`stelow-product-pricing`), forms of guarantee, and bonuses adding complementary value
(see `stelow-product-promotions`).

## Find and focus on acquisition channels

1. Experiment with several promotion channels simultaneously and evaluate performance.
2. Select one or two with the best result — not necessarily the biggest volume, but the
   **most aligned customers** — and double down on them.
3. Once one channel is stable, start experimenting with others.

## What risk this mitigates

- **Execution / realization risk** — feasibility in the available time, skills and
  technologies
- **Usability risk** — whether people will know how to use the new solution
- **Business risk** — basic viability: whether you can sell enough and earn enough to
  cover operational expenses and generate profit

## What to observe (signals)

These are lenses, not goals:

- **Proxy metric** — the main action indicating users extract value. E.g. the percentage
  of new customers who create their first project in week one. Answers: *are people using
  the solution for its main purpose?*
- **Retention rate** — how many customers keep using the solution over time. Answers: *do
  people keep coming back?*
- **CLTV to CAC ratio** — over the long run, is the value a customer generates greater
  than the cost of acquiring them? Points to financial sustainability.
- **Viral coefficient** — are current customers bringing new ones? Answers: *does the
  solution spread organically?*

For the full signal-tension framework, see `stelow-product-health`.

## It is not the end — evolutionary product

This stage begins a new learning cycle with a more complete system running. Experimentation
continues, now focused on optimizing and adapting the business to a changing environment.
The evolutionary product criteria — in `stelow-product-evolutionary-principles` — remain
the compass: judge each new feature or adjustment not only by its immediate merit but by
whether it opens new paths and strengthens resilience.

## When to Use & Test Cases

Should activate: "how do I turn this into a business?", "what should I build first?",
"which channel should I double down on?", "do I have product-market fit?".

Should NOT activate: "how do I validate the idea" (early stages), "how do I deliver
manually" (manual-process), "what should I charge" (pricing).

## Examples

### Example: course business after a manual cohort

**Input:** "I ran two cohorts by hand. What do I build?"

**Steps:**
1. Four fits: market-product evidenced (two cohorts paid), model-market assumed,
   channel-model unknown, product-channel unknown.
2. Stepping stone: systematize the core value — automate scheduling and feedback
   collection only. Postpones the video platform entirely.
3. Channel: warm referrals won two of the last ten sign-ups, so focus there first.

**Output:** four fits marked, one stepping stone, one channel.

### Example: vanity growth

**Input:** "We grew 300% last month, we must be at product-market fit."

**Steps:**
1. Growth is not a fit. Check retention before celebrating.
2. Read the proxy metric: are new users doing the main action in week one?
3. If retention is flat while acquisition spikes, the channel is buying curiosity.

**Output:** retention verdict before any scaling decision.

## Edge Cases

### All four fits look green
- Suspect the measurement, not your luck. Name what evidence would falsify each fit.

### Channel works only with the founder personally selling
- That is a channel-model mismatch, not success. Either make the founder's motion
  teachable or pick a channel that does not need them.

### Revenue arrives before retention
- Fine short-term, dangerous long-term. Keep the retention lens active while the cash
  funds the next cycle.

### Everything must launch at once
- The stepping-stone idea exists to make that unnecessary. Pick the stone that opens the
  most next steps, not the one that looks most complete.
